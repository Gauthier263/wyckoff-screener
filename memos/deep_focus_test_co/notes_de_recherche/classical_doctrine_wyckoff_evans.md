## 1. What is the exact Composite Man passage, and in which Wyckoff/SMI text does it appear?

### Takeaway
The canonical passage is in Wyckoff's own correspondence course, **Section 9M, Page 1** ("How a Campaign is Conducted"), on a page carrying "Copyright 1931 by Richard D. Wyckoff" (re-stamped 1937 by Wyckoff Associates) — i.e. written by Wyckoff himself in 1931, not by SMI. The companion tape-reading division (©1931–32) uses the variant **"Composite Operator" (abbreviated "C. O.")** and explicitly says "There is no Composite Operator" — it is a heuristic for the aggregate of bankers, pools, operators, floor traders and public.

### Cited Findings
- **Exact passage (Course §9M, p.1, page ©1931 RDW / ©1937 Wyckoff Associates):** "Like individuals, stocks have certain characteristics with which one becomes more and more familiar as he studies intensively their past and current movements. The market is made by the minds of men, and all the fluctuations in the market and in all the various stocks should be studied as if they were the result of one man's operations. Let us call him the Composite Man, who, in theory, sits behind the scenes and manipulates the stocks to your disadvantage if you do not understand the game as he plays it; and to your great profit if you do understand it." — [Wyckoff Course 1937 ed., §9M p.1](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Immediately following (same page):** "Not all of the manipulators' moves can be detected. Not all of the moves are made by manipulators. In fact it does not matter to the tape reader or the chart reader whether the moves are real or artificial; that is, the result of actual buying and selling by the public and bona fide investors or artificial buying and selling by large operators. Most of the important moves in the market are prepared, executed and concluded, and it is my business to show you how a large number of these trading and investment opportunities may be spotted in time to take advantage of them." — [Course §9M p.1](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Preceding sentences (same page) — the "sponsor" idea:** "To do this requires a knowledge of what goes on behind the scenes. A stock carries the earmarks of its chief sponsors. Its action usually indicates the character, methods and ability of those who operate heaviest in it." — [Course §9M p.1](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Ethical aside (§9M p.3, ©1931):** "You say all this is unethical, if not unscrupulous. You say it is a cruel and crooked game. Very well. Electricity can be very cruel, but you can take advantage of it; you can make it work for your benefit. Just so with the stock market and the Composite Man. Play the game as he plays it. I am telling you the rules. I am giving you the inside view." — [Course §9M p.3](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **"Composite Operator" variant (Tape Reading division, TR §9 p.4, page ©1931 RDW):** "Imagine, if you can, that the whole market is controlled by what we may call the Composite Operator, and that every move is either the result of his operations or of those influenced by him." — [Wyckoff, Tape Reading & Active Trading (©1932), TR §9 p.4](https://archive.org/details/TheRichardD.WyckoffMethodOfTradingInStocks)
- **Wyckoff's own disclaimer (TR §10 pp.4–5, ©1932):** "There is no Composite Operator, but the effect of the combined operations of bankers, pools, large operators, floor traders and the public is, when boiled down on the tape, of the same effect as if it were produced by one man's operations." He then grounds it in "the fundamental law of supply and demand, which is at the bottom of every move ... It does not matter whether the buying and the selling, or both, are genuine or artificial; that is, manipulative; designed for a purpose." — [TR §10 p.5](https://archive.org/details/TheRichardD.WyckoffMethodOfTradingInStocks)
- **Third wording, post-1934 rationale (Course §11M p.1):** understanding manipulative procedure "helps us to judge the motives, the hopes, fears and aspirations of all the buyers and sellers whose actions today have the same net effect upon the market as so many pool operations would have. So if we are squeamish about the term 'manipulator' we may substitute the words 'Composite Operator'". — [Course §11M p.1](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **1937 footnote acknowledging the legal ban (§9M p.2, page ©1931/©1937; content clearly post-1934):** "Some people might object to this statement on the ground that regulation of the stock market has eliminated pool operations. Even though pool operations and old-fashioned manipulation are banned by law, for our purpose in studying, understanding and correctly interpreting market action, we must consider any operation a 'manufactured' movement wherein the buying or the selling is sufficiently concerted and coming from interests better informed than the public as to produce the same effects as pure manipulation." — [Course §9M p.2, footnote](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Modern citation of the passage:** StockCharts ChartSchool (Bogomazov & Lipsett, ed. Pruden) quotes the same text and cites "The Richard D. Wyckoff Course in Stock Market Science and Technique, section 9, p. 1-2". — [StockCharts, The Wyckoff Method: A Tutorial](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/market-analysis/wyckoff-analysis-articles/the-wyckoff-method-a-tutorial)
- **SMI glossary definition:** "Composite Man: The term used to refer to the sponsors or large professional interests in the stock market, also called composite operator." — [Wyckoff SMI, Library/Glossary](https://wyckoffsmi.com/library/)
- **Not present earlier:** in *Studies in Tape Reading* (1910) "composite" only appears as "composite chart" (an average of leading stocks); in *How I Trade* (1922) only as "the composite that was presented to me" (aggregate order flow at a wire house) and "the combined or composite opinion of the millions" — no "Composite Man/Operator". — [STR 1910, ch. III](https://archive.org/details/studiesintaperea00wyckrich); [How I Trade, p.26](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031269552)

### Inferences
- The named personification "Composite Man/Operator" first appears (in the texts searched) in the 1931–32 course; the *idea* is older: in 1922 Wyckoff describes, as a wire-house clerk, judging customers only "by the composite that was presented to me", and in 1910 he says it "is a matter of indifference to the Tape Reader as to who or what produces these tests" (see §2).
- The Course uses "Composite Man" (9M) and the tape division "Composite Operator"/"C. O." (TR 9–10) interchangeably; TR §10 p.6 even lists "the Composite Man, a pool manager, large operator, or a big floor trader" side by side. Treat both labels as Wyckoff's own.
- Wyckoff frames the C.O. as a didactic fiction from the start (1932: "There is no Composite Operator"), and the 1937 revision re-justifies it after manipulation became illegal: "concerted" informed flows produce "the same effects as pure manipulation". This is the defensible framing for a modern (crypto/gold) memo.

### Gaps
- The 1931 first-edition pages were not seen; the scan is the 1937 edition. Page-level "©1931 Richard D. Wyckoff" lines strongly suggest the 9M p.1 wording is Wyckoff's 1931 text, but word-for-word identity with the 1931 printing is unverified (StockCharts' "p. 1-2" vs this scan's "p. 1" may reflect a different printing).

## 2. What did Wyckoff (and Lefèvre/Livermore) actually write about operators testing the market before campaigns?

### Takeaway
"Testing" is explicit, recurrent vocabulary in Wyckoff (1910, 1931–32) and in Lefèvre/Livermore (1923). The classical test is an **action** by a large player — buying or selling a probe quantity, marking up a leader, driving stocks down, withdrawing support — followed by reading the market's **response** (how easily stock is bought, how selling is absorbed, whether others follow). Wyckoff's key pedagogical move: the small trader cannot afford tests, but can read the big players' tests on the tape "as if you had made them yourself".

### Cited Findings
**2a. Wyckoff, *Studies in Tape Reading* (1910, as "Rollo Tape")**
- Tests as critical junctures (ch. VI "Market Technique", pp.102–103): "The market is a tug-o'-war. Successful tape reading requires ability to judge which side has the greatest pulling power ... There are critical points which occur in each swing ... At these junctures it seems as though a feather's weight on either side would determine the immediate trend." ... "It is a matter of indifference to the Tape Reader as to who or what produces these tests, or critical periods. They constantly appear and disappear; he must make his diagnosis and act accordingly." — [STR 1910, pp.102–103](https://archive.org/details/studiesintaperea00wyckrich)
- Floor traders as permanent testers (ch. VI, p.105): "The market is being put to the test continually by one element of which little has been said, viz., the floor traders. These shrewd fellows are always on the alert to ferret out a weak spot in the market, for they love the short side. Lack of support, if detected, in an issue generally leads to a raid ... Or, if they find a vulnerable short interest, they are quick to bid up a stock and drive the shorts to cover. ... Other people are doing for him what he would do himself if he were all-powerful." — [STR 1910, p.105](https://archive.org/details/studiesintaperea00wyckrich)
- Offering stock and meeting absorption (ch. V "Volumes and Their Significance", p.81): a broker "pounding" a stock who meets "strong resistance at 79 ... showing that at 79 there was a demand for more than he was willing to supply (there might have been 10,000 shares wanted at 79). Frequently a broker meeting such an obstacle will leave the crowd long enough to 'phone his principal. His departure opens the way for a rally, as the stock is no longer under pressure". — [STR 1910, p.81](https://archive.org/details/studiesintaperea00wyckrich)
- Pool tactics on the floor (p.82): "Take another case where two brokers are put into the crowd — one to depress the stock and the other to accumulate it. They play into each other's hands". — [STR 1910, p.82](https://archive.org/details/studiesintaperea00wyckrich)
- Campaign structure (pp.91–92): "the manipulator is obliged to deal in large blocks of stock and must continually show his hand. A complete manipulative operation on the long side consists of three parts: Accumulation, marking up, and distribution. In the case of a short operation the distribution comes first, then the mark down and the accumulation. ... (For full details see my series on Manipulations in Vols. 1 and 2 of The Ticker.)" — [STR 1910, pp.91–92](https://archive.org/details/studiesintaperea00wyckrich)
- Pools (ch. III, p.50): "The absence of inside manipulation in a stock leaves the way open for pools to operate, and many of the moves that are observed in these groups are produced by a handful of floor or office operators, who, by joining hands and swinging large quantities, are able to force their stock in the desired direction." — [STR 1910, p.50](https://archive.org/details/studiesintaperea00wyckrich)
- A personal timing test (p.108): "I have frequently used this idea as a test of the market's strength or weakness" (a rally lasting ten minutes instead of the normal half hour = weakness). — [STR 1910, p.108](https://archive.org/details/studiesintaperea00wyckrich)

**2b. Wyckoff, *How I Trade and Invest in Stocks and Bonds* (1922)**
- Pools' modus operandi (ch. VI, pp.98–99): "the campaigns mapped out and carried out by pools consisting of groups of a few or many men who look far ahead ... Pools work on this weakness, which is due to ignorance on the part of the public. They accumulate a stock without advancing its price; then, when market conditions are favorable, they bid the stock up. This excites public buying ... Vice versa, groups will often try to depress a stock, counting on the public's support when the issue begins to decline." — [How I Trade, pp.98–99](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031269552)
- "Large interests are practically always in the market. They usually have their scale orders in on both sides so that they buy on declines and sell on rallies." (p.100) — [How I Trade, p.100](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031269552)
- Method: "my object being to find out or reason out what the large operator did and how he did it; then I could operate in the same way" (p.40). — [How I Trade, p.40](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031269552)
- Wyckoff's 1909 realization, quoted by the Course from *Wall Street Ventures and Adventures Through Forty Years* (1930), "pg. 168": "I saw more and more that the action of stocks reflected the plans and purposes of those who dominated them. I began to see possibilities of judging from the very tape what these master minds were doing." — [Course §1M p.2](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)

**2c. The Course (1931; 1937 ed.), "M" sections**
- **The explicit testing passage (§8M p.1; page ©1931/©1937, contains 1936–37 examples so partly revised):** "Large operators often test the market for a stock by buying 5,000 to 25,000 shares in order to see how easily they can buy it, or by selling a similar quantity to ascertain how well the market will absorb their selling. Thus they are able to decide which side shows the least resistance. If they find other people are trying to buy it and that the stock is rather scarce, they regard it as a bullish indication and take a long position. Or, if the price yields easily to pressure, they regard it as a bearish indication and take a short position. A small operator is unable to test the market in this way before he takes a position, but by a close study of his charts, he is able to estimate the comparative strength or weakness of a stock". — [Course §8M p.1](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- Same page: "When large interests are planning a campaign, in a stock, they 'lay the foundation.' That is, they accumulate or distribute a quantity according to the size of their venture and the anticipated profit". — [Course §8M p.1](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- Gauging supply overhead (§14M p.4, ©1931): "The operator opposes an advance ... until he has secured all the stock he believes he can get. His opposition consists of large selling orders placed with several brokers and through them with the specialists; so that it appears that a lot of the stock is for sale just above the present market. Floor traders and outside operators who work on stock market technique make inquiries before they buy any large quantity of stock such as: 'How much is offered in the next five points up?' And while the specialists are not supposed to give out this information, it is often obtainable." — [Course §14M p.4](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- "A Typical Market Operation" (§9M pp.3–6, ©1931): accumulation by scale orders 33→30, then "he forces the price down to around 30 by offering large amounts of stock and inducing floor traders and other people to sell their long holdings or go short because the stock looks weak"; to accumulate "he raids the market for that stock, makes it look very weak, and gives it the appearance of heavy liquidation by sending in selling orders through a great number of brokers"; at the top he keeps "good-sized supporting orders in around 56 to fool the floor traders, the specialists and the public ... When the operator has sold all of his 25,000 shares short, he cancels all of his buying orders. The specialist in the stock then tells some of the more important floor traders that the stock is in a weak technical position and that there is no support for the next 8 or 10 points and they all get together and raid it down to 50". — [Course §9M pp.2–6](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- Testing support in a range (§15M p.8): "the appearance of a secondary reaction which will test the preliminary support around 44-45. This test may confirm the previous support (show that a good quality of demand exists around these levels), or it may show that support has been withdrawn". (§16M p.7): "it has successfully met a test of the support. In other words, it has shown by its action that there is still a good quality of demand between 10 and 11". — [Course §15M p.8, §16M p.7](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- Livermore as Wyckoff's informant (§9M p.3): "Livermore once told me he never touched anything unless there were at least 10 points in it according to his calculations." — [Course §9M p.3](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)

**2d. Tape Reading division (©1931–32): "JUDGING THE MARKET BY TESTS AND RESPONSES" (TR §9 p.4 – §10 p.6) — the single most direct primary text on the C.O. testing**
- Opening: "Manipulators are constantly testing the market to see whether it is most responsive on the bull or the bear side. They want to know whether they can get a following in the industrial, rail or utilities group, or in any other group or stock representing a certain industry." — [TR §9 p.4](https://archive.org/details/TheRichardD.WyckoffMethodOfTradingInStocks)
- The test by mark-up of a leader: "Now suppose, at the opening of the market today, this C. O. wants to know whether the temper of the public is bullish or bearish. First he tests out the rails by advancing Union Pacific one or two points. He observes how much response is given, as indicated by advances in other rails. If none of these follows his lead ... he decides that he cannot get a following in that group for the time being." — [TR §9 p.4](https://archive.org/details/TheRichardD.WyckoffMethodOfTradingInStocks)
- Sequential probing: "Next he tests out Steel, Telephone or Can in the same way. He gets some following in Can, a little in Telephone and nothing much in Steel. Then he tries out the utility group ... From this series of tests he now knows that he can best advance the market by bulling Can. ... Observe that he follows the line of least resistance. He avoids bulling the rails because he would there have to play a lone hand. ... After a while the small bulges which he has produced in his testing operations, followed by the more active bidding up of a larger number of stocks, encourage floor traders and the public to get in on the bull side." — [TR §10 p.1](https://archive.org/details/TheRichardD.WyckoffMethodOfTradingInStocks)
- Reaccumulation by absorption, and shakeout-drives as tests: "Out of 1,000 shares offered at a certain price he may take 600 or 700 leaving 300 still offered and the market unchanged. His reaccumulation is not apparent. ... he drives several stocks downward to weaken the market so he can complete his purchases on the reaction. These drives are also his tests." Then "the market is now in a position technically known as 'sold out'". — [TR §10 pp.2–3](https://archive.org/details/TheRichardD.WyckoffMethodOfTradingInStocks)
- Breaking a "double top" by testing latent demand: "the C. O. finds that the long selling combined with the short selling is not sufficient to stop the advance of the market. There is a latent buying power that appears to increase on advances. He bids certain stocks up to new highs in order to encourage this outside buying power." — [TR §10 p.3](https://archive.org/details/TheRichardD.WyckoffMethodOfTradingInStocks)
- News as tests: "The market is also continually being tested by the various news items and developments ... A test of the strength or weakness of any stock, or any kind of a market, is frequently afforded by these." (Allied Chemical, June 1932: dividend declared, "half-hearted rally to 47 ... This afforded a test of the temper of the market, which was bearish.") — [TR §10 pp.5–6](https://archive.org/details/TheRichardD.WyckoffMethodOfTradingInStocks)
- The free-rider principle: "One more word about these tests. No matter who makes them — the Composite Man, a pool manager, large operator, or a big floor trader, the result of the tests show immediately on the tape. You, sitting at the ticker, can observe at once whether these tests bring a bullish or bearish response; and you are just as free to act upon them as if you had made them yourself." — [TR §10 p.6](https://archive.org/details/TheRichardD.WyckoffMethodOfTradingInStocks)
- The division's syllabus line: "How the operator tests the technical condition of the market. How the individual trader may secure, without expense, the benefit of the large operator's tests of the market's strength." — [TR course, section summaries](https://archive.org/details/TheRichardD.WyckoffMethodOfTradingInStocks)

**2e. Lefèvre, *Reminiscences* (1923) — Livermore's own tests**
- The "testing order" (ch. VII): after pyramiding, "if I get that four thousand at 113¾ I know something is wrong and I'll give a testing order--that is, I'll sell one thousand shares to see how the market takes it." Same chapter: "He has to sell when he can, not when he wants to. To learn the time, he has to watch and test." — [Reminiscences, ch. VII](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)
- Cost of probing (ch. X): "In the course of accumulating my full line I might chip out fifty or sixty thousand dollars in these feeling-out plays of mine. This looks like a very expensive testing, but it wasn't." and "I check up my own trading by additional tests, to determine the psychological moment. I do that by watching the way the price acts after I begin." — [Reminiscences, ch. X](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)
- (ch. XIV): "minor losses such as I often incurred in testing the market before putting down the big bet." — [Reminiscences, ch. XIV](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)
- The wheat test — reading absorption (ch. XVII): "On this day when I sold the 250,000 bushels to test the market for timeliness, the price went down ¼ cent. ... I noticed that it was taken in driblets; that is, the buying was in lots of 10,000 or 15,000 bushels instead of being taken in two or three transactions which would have been the normal way. ... the way in which the market took my wheat and the disproportionate decline on my selling told me that there was no buying power there." He then sold 2,000,000 bushels. — [Reminiscences, ch. XVII](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)
- (ch. XVIII): "So I tested the market again and again, in accordance with my trading methods"; "The entire market was entitled to a good reaction ... it did not bull any longer. I know because I tested it." — [Reminiscences, ch. XVIII](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)
- Room traders testing a pool (ch. VIII, Reading bull pool): "Every now and then the room traders looked at the price, smacked their lips and proceeded to test it with a thousand shares or two. They could not dislodge a share, so they covered and went looking elsewhere for easier money." — [Reminiscences, ch. VIII](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)
- Note: the words "probe/probing" do not occur in the Gutenberg text (full-text search); Livermore's vocabulary is "test", "testing order", "feeling-out plays". — [Reminiscences full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/60979/pg60979.txt)

**2f. Lefèvre — Keene and the manipulation chapters (XIX–XXII)**
- **Withdrawing support to see if the stock holds by itself (ch. XX, Amalgamated Copper, via one of Keene's "confidential men"):** "Keene would find himself almost out of stock one day ... and on the next day he would buy back thousands of shares. On the day after that, he would sell on balance. Then he would leave the market absolutely alone, to see how it would take care of itself and also to accustom it to do so. When it came to the actual marketing of the line he did what I told you: he sold it on the way down." — [Reminiscences, ch. XX](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)
- Keene's rule: "Keene worked as he always did--that is, doing his big selling on the way down after the big rise." Lefèvre adds "We can't analyse Keene's manipulative campaigns. His books are not available; the adequately detailed record is nonexistent." — [Reminiscences, ch. XX](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)
- Buying as a test of demand (ch. XX, Livermore on his own manipulation): "When my buying does not put the stock up I stop buying and then proceed to sell it down"; and floor traders probing for absent bids: "some sharp-eyed trader may perceive that there are no buying orders to speak of in my stock, and he sells it, and his fellows follow." — [Reminiscences, ch. XX](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)
- Conspicuous bidding as a double bluff (ch. XX): Gould's broker Washington E. Connor "began to bid for Western Union. The traders to a man laughed--at his stupidity in thinking them so simple--and they cheerfully sold him all the stock he wanted to buy." — [Reminiscences, ch. XX](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)
- Mapping supply overhead before a campaign (ch. XXI, Imperial Steel): "The first thing I did was to find out how much stock was likely to come on the market on an advance. This was easily done through my brokers, who had no trouble in ascertaining what stock was for sale at or a little above the market." Then: "When I had absorbed all the stock that was for sale at 70 and a little higher I relieved the market of that pressure, and naturally that made clear for trading purposes the line of least resistance in Imperial Steel. It was manifestly upward." — [Reminiscences, ch. XXI](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)
- Pools test public capacity (ch. XXII): Jim Barnes's pool "did rather more than merely overestimate the public's buying capacity. After all, that capacity was something that they could determine only by actual tests." — [Reminiscences, ch. XXII](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)
- Livermore minimizing wash sales (ch. XXI): "People lay great stress on old-time practices, such as wash sales. But I can assure you that the mere mechanics of deception count for very little." — [Reminiscences, ch. XXI](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/60979)

**2g. The "laws" that ground the logic of tests**
- The Course names only one law explicitly: "THE BASIC LAW OF SUPPLY AND DEMAND" (§1M p.2); TR §10 p.5 calls it "the fundamental law of supply and demand, which is at the bottom of every move". Full-text search of the 1937 course finds 0 hits for "cause and effect" and for "effort and/vs/versus result". — [Course §1M p.2](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading); [TR §10 p.5](https://archive.org/details/TheRichardD.WyckoffMethodOfTradingInStocks)
- The "three laws" packaging (supply & demand; cause & effect; effort vs result) is how modern teachers present the method: StockCharts lists "The law of supply and demand", "The law of cause and effect", "The law of effort versus result"; Pruden & von Lichtenstein (2006) refer to an STA article (Nov 2004, Issue 51) giving "check lists" for the "three laws" and "nine tests". — [StockCharts tutorial](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/market-analysis/wyckoff-analysis-articles/the-wyckoff-method-a-tutorial); [Pruden & von Lichtenstein, Market Technician 55 (2006)](https://www.wyckoffanalytics.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/WyckoffSchematics-VisualTemplatesForMarketTimingDecisions.pdf)
- The cause idea exists in the Course without the label: the accumulated quantity "bears a relation to the estimated number of points profit. (Figure charts afford a means of judging this relation...)" (§8M pp.1–2). — [Course §8M](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)

### Inferences
- Classical taxonomy of tests, as documented 1910–1932: (1) **probe-buy** to see how easily stock can be bought / whether supply is scarce (8M p.1; Livermore's buying rule ch. XX); (2) **probe-sell** to see how selling is absorbed (8M p.1; Livermore's "testing order", wheat ch. XVII); (3) **mark-up of a leader** to test for a following (TR §9–10, Union Pacific/Can); (4) **drive/raid down** to shake out and test residual supply ("These drives are also his tests", TR §10 p.2; terminal shake-out, see §5); (5) **withdrawal of support** to see if the stock holds by itself (Keene, ch. XX; cancellation of buying orders, 9M p.5); (6) **inquiry into the book** ("How much is offered in the next five points up?", 14M p.4; Livermore ch. XXI); (7) **exogenous tests** (news, TR §10 pp.5–6). Types 1–5 are effort-vs-response readings even though Wyckoff never calls them "effort vs result".
- In this classical usage, "test" is broader than the modern Wyckoff "test" (low-volume revisit of a spring/SC area): it is any probe whose *response* reveals the balance of supply and demand, and the modern ST/spring-test is one special case (cf. StockCharts: "Large operators always test the market for supply throughout a TR (e.g., STs and springs)").
- Testing was considered expensive and therefore a big-player privilege (Livermore's $50–60k "feeling-out plays"; 8M p.1 "A small operator is unable to test the market in this way") — which is exactly why Wyckoff teaches reading others' tests.

### Gaps
- *Wall Street Ventures and Adventures* (1930) could not be read (archive.org copies are lending-only, HTTP 401 on text); only the course's quotation of p.168 is available. Wyckoff's own anecdotes of operators testing in that memoir are therefore unverified.
- *Stock Market Technique* (Wyckoff's 1933–34 compilations) was not located online; not checked.

## 3. How did Wyckoff/SMI define the "springboard" and the idea that a stock is "on the springboard"? Is it truly Wyckoff's term?

### Takeaway
Yes — "springboard" is Wyckoff's own term, used 30+ times in the 1931–37 course, with a formal definition in §14M pp.3–5 (©1931) and "out on the springboard, as we call it" in §24M. Crucially, Wyckoff locates the springboard **inside the range, at the end of preparation** ("at the bottom of a range of accumulation, or in the upper levels of a range of distribution"), explicitly rejecting the view that it is the breakout. SMI's glossary keeps the term ("a stock that has completed preparation...").

### Cited Findings
- **Definition (§14M pp.3–4, ©1931):** "When a stock is 'on the springboard': On the bull side a stock (or a group, or the market as a whole) is in this position following a period of preparation. This usually occurs at the bottom of a decline — though it also occurs after the price has been in a trading range, following consolidation of a previous advance in preparation for a new mark-up." — [Course §14M pp.3–4](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **The operator's role (§14M pp.4–5):** "A stock is on the springboard on the bull side when the operator has completed his original line and is ready to let the stock advance ... Up to that moment the supply has been artificial. He is ready to allow the demand to overcome supply. At this stage this is comparatively easy because he has mopped up all the floating supply at that level. Of all the times to take a position in a stock this is the best." — [Course §14M pp.4–5](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Bear side (§14M p.5):** "A stock (or a group, or the market as a whole) is on the springboard on the bear side, of course, following a period of preparation, in this case distribution. This usually occurs at the top of an advance, but it also occurs after the price has been in a trading range, following a decline, in which further distribution is taking place in preparation for a new downward plunge." — [Course §14M p.5](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Not the breakout (§14M p.5):** "Some people regard a stock (or the market) in this (springboard) position only when it breaks through an old line of resistance or support into a higher or lower field. I claim that the beginning of the springboard move is at the bottom of a range of accumulation, or in the upper levels of a range of distribution." — [Course §14M p.5](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- "Nearly every day some stocks are on the springboard ready for a sharp move up ... or prepared for a plunge downward". — [Course §14M p.5](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Wyckoff's possessive usage (§24M p.15):** "when it is out on the springboard, as we call it, and is giving almost positive evidence that the psychological moment has arrived for a pyramiding play." (§24M p.1): "Wait until it gets to the end of its period of preparation and is out on the end of the springboard, ready to go." — [Course §24M](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Tied to hinge / dead center / apex (§10M p.3):** "the stock is on the springboard for it has oscillated to a dead center, or hinge, at 30 ... and appears to have completed preparation for an important advance. Now if volume increases as the stock recovers, you will have a final confirmation". (§7M p.23 fn.): "The average is now on the hinge and on the 'Springboard' for an important slump." (§20M p.3): "the stock develops a perfect apex (note how the price movement and volume have narrowed into extreme dullness). A rally out of this hinge position will easily penetrate the supply line A-B (Buying Test 5) and put the stock on the springboard". — [Course §10M p.3, §7M p.23, §20M p.3](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **"Second springboard position" after the first push (§11M p.6):** "After a small dip to 54 it develops the second springboard position at 56 (S) for a probable fast mark-up". (§16M p.21): "the range again promptly narrows and volume immediately falls off during a shallow recession, telling us in advance that the offerings around those old tops were either dislodged ... or they are being absorbed — the stock is in the second springboard position from whence we should expect the marking up phase to develop actively." — [Course §11M p.6, §16M p.21](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Confirmation by volume (§21M p.1, ©1932):** "Very shortly after that it got on the springboard at 47, then bulged to 49 and had a three point reaction which gave a splendid buying opportunity with a close stop. The springboard position was confirmed by ability of the stock to rise to 50 and above on increasing volume." — [Course §21M p.1](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Purpose of the charts (§4M p.5, page ©1936):** volume and price movement help "(c) when a stock is on the springboard, and (d) when a move is culminating"; figure charts help "(d) when a stock is on the springboard" (§4M p.6). — [Course §4M](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **SMI glossary (paraphrase by fetch tool, not verbatim):** springboard = a stock that has finished preparing and is positioned to move into markup or markdown; verbatim opening: "A condition in the price movement of a stock that has completed preparation and has been brought to a point" (truncated). — [Wyckoff SMI Library/Glossary](https://wyckoffsmi.com/library/)
- **Modern usage:** a Wyckoff Analytics/Gary Fullett course blurb: the appearance of a "springboard" at the right edge of a chart "signals that a stock ... is (finally) poised to move out of a trading range". — [Wyckoff Analytics, Fullett May 2019](https://www.wyckoffanalytics.com/may-2019-fullett)

### Inferences
- Wyckoff's 1931 springboard is functionally the ancestor of several later SMI labels: its first position (end of preparation at the bottom of the range, after shake-out/dullness/hinge) ≈ the modern spring/test/LPS-inside-the-range zone; its "second springboard position" (shallow, low-volume recession after a push through old tops) ≈ the later Evans "back-up to the edge of the creek"/LPS.
- Because Wyckoff ties the springboard to the moment the operator "is ready to allow the demand to overcome supply" after artificial supply is withdrawn, "springboard" is the *outcome* of successful tests (supply mopped up), not a test itself.
- Modern usage that equates springboard with a breakout contradicts Wyckoff's explicit wording ("I claim...").

### Gaps
- Whether Wyckoff used "springboard" before 1931 (e.g., in *The Ticker*/*Magazine of Wall Street* 1907–1926) was not checked (not in *Studies in Tape Reading* 1910: 0 hits).
- A 1968 SMI course unit reportedly titled "Accumulation with a Perfect Apex and a Perfect Springboard" appeared only in a search result (Scribd, unverified) — not opened.

## 4. What exactly is the canonical list and order of the Nine Buying Tests / Nine Selling Tests, and where do they originate?

### Takeaway
The earliest text found is in the Wyckoff Course itself, **§20M p.1 ("BUYING AND SELLING TESTS"), on pages copyrighted "1937 by Wyckoff Associates, Inc." only** (introduced on §19M p.14) — i.e. the summary table belongs to the 1937 post-Wyckoff revision; its presence in the 1931–33 original is unverified. The modern list (Pruden 2007, reproduced by StockCharts) **merges SC and ST into test 3 and reorders the buying tests**, while keeping the 1937 selling order. For fidelity to the original, cite the 1937 order.

### Cited Findings
- **Purpose (§20M p.1, ©1937 Wyckoff Associates):** "These buying and selling tests outline in a general way the successive steps to be taken when determining the most advantageous buying and selling points with respect to the market as a whole and with respect to individual stocks or groups of stocks." — [Course §20M p.1](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **BUYING TESTS (§20M p.1, verbatim; "Indication — Determined From"):** 1. "Objective Accomplished on Down Side — Figure Chart"; 2. "Activity Bullish — volume increasing normally on rallies and decreasing on reactions — Vertical Chart"; 3. "Preliminary Support — Vertical and Figure Charts"; 4. "Stronger Than Market — responsive to rallies and resistant to reactions — Vertical Chart"; 5. "Downward Stride Broken, i.e., supply lines penetrated — Vertical or Figure Charts"; 6. "Higher Supports — Vertical or Figure Charts"; 7. "Higher Tops — Vertical or Figure Charts"; 8. "Base Forming — Figure Chart"; 9. "Estimated Probable Profit Exceeds Indicated Probable Risk — Figure Chart for objective. Vertical Chart for placing stop." — [Course §20M p.1](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **SELLING TESTS (§20M p.1, verbatim; OCR "5." before Preliminary Supply is a misread of "3."):** 1. "Objective Accomplished on Up Side — Figure Chart"; 2. "Activity Bearish — volume increasing normally on reactions and decreasing on rallies — Vertical Chart"; 3. "Preliminary Supply — Vertical and Figure Charts"; 4. "Weaker Than Market — responsive to reactions and sluggish on rallies — Vertical Chart"; 5. "Upward Stride Broken, i.e., support lines violated — Vertical, or Figure Charts"; 6. "Lower Tops"; 7. "Lower Supports"; 8. "Crown Forming (lateral movement) — Figure Chart"; 9. "Estimated Probable Profits Exceed Indicated Risk". — [Course §20M p.1](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **3:1 rule and scope (§20M p.2, ©1937):** "The above buying tests would ordinarily be applied to an average or to a stock after a decline and the selling tests after an advance. Before making any commitment, always make sure that the estimated probable profit as determined by the figure chart objective, is at least three times the risk involved in employing a properly placed stop. The vertical chart must be used to determine the logical points at which to place stop orders." — [Course §20M p.2](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Worked example (Atchison 1932, §20M pp.2–4):** shake-out appearance, then "An abnormal expansion of volume ... indicates the climax of the movement and the development of Preliminary Support — Buying Test No. 3"; "the stock at 18 has fulfilled the objective ... — Buying Test No. 1"; "A quick rally to 20 3/8 on comparatively light volume implies a scarcity of offerings and confirms previous indications ... by breaking the downward stride ... — Buying Test No. 5." — [Course §20M pp.2–3](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Modern version (StockCharts, "Adapted from Pruden H (2007) The Three Skills of Top Trading ... pp. 136-37"):** Buying: 1 Downside price objective accomplished (P&F); **2 "Preliminary support, selling climax, secondary test"**; 3 Activity bullish; 4 Downward stride broken; 5 Higher lows; 6 Higher highs; **7 Stock stronger than the market**; 8 Base forming (horizontal price line); 9 Upside profit potential at least three times the loss if the initial stop-loss were hit. Selling: 1 Upside objective accomplished; 2 Activity bearish; **3 "Preliminary supply, buying climax"**; 4 Stock weaker than the market; 5 Upward stride broken; 6 Lower highs; 7 Lower lows; 8 Crown forming (lateral movement); 9 three-times rule. — [StockCharts tutorial](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/market-analysis/wyckoff-analysis-articles/the-wyckoff-method-a-tutorial)
- StockCharts' illustration confirms the merge: "The downtrend in this example of AAPL concludes with Preliminary Support (PS), a Selling Climax (SC), an Automatic Rally (AR) and a Secondary Test (ST), which combine to satisfy Test #2." and frames the tests as defining "the line of least resistance in the market". — [StockCharts tutorial](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/market-analysis/wyckoff-analysis-articles/the-wyckoff-method-a-tutorial)
- SMI's own glossary mentions "the nine buying and selling tests" (under "Selecting A Stock") without a list in the fetched portion. — [Wyckoff SMI Library](https://wyckoffsmi.com/library/)
- A Scribd table (search result, not opened) credits a version of the tests to Hutson's compilation "with modifications" — unverified. — [Scribd "Law Test" (search result)](https://www.scribd.com/doc/40206785/Law-Test)

## 5. Terminology origins (test, springboard, spring, shakeout/terminal shakeout, secondary test, back-up, creek, ice, jump across the creek) — did Evans coin them, and is the back-up presented as an LPS?

### Takeaway
Full-text search of the 1931–37 Wyckoff course shows Wyckoff's own vocabulary was **test, springboard, hinge/apex, preliminary support/supply, selling climax, buying climax, technical rally, secondary reaction, shake-out, terminal shake-out, upthrust/thrust, mark-up**. **"Spring" (as an event), "secondary test", "automatic rally", "sign of strength", "back-up", "creek", "ice", "jump across the creek" do not occur in it.** Creek/JAC/back-up/ice are credited to **Robert G. Evans** (Pruden; StockCharts; SMI), and SMI says the spring "is a refinement of Mr. Wyckoff's concept of a Terminal Shake-Out". "Terminal shake-out" is **Wyckoff's own** (§21M, ©1932), not Evans'. Pruden and StockCharts explicitly present the back-up as a pullback **to an LPS**; Evans' framing (via Pruden) is that "a backup is a test".

- **Terminal shake-out — Wyckoff's definition (§21M p.1, ©1932 RDW):** "A Terminal Shake-out is a rapid or precipitous downward movement, occurring at or near the end of a period of preparation for an advance. In the case of deliberate manipulation, the purpose of the terminal shake-out is to scare holders of stock into selling out; to catch stops which may have been placed on long positions below the previous line of supports in the accumulation zone, in other words, mop up as much cheap stock as possible; and to encourage short selling around the bottom on the part of the public. After the bag has thus been held for the weak holders and amateur shorts, the strings are pulled to lock in these shorts and to shut out the sold out bulls." And: "It makes no practical difference whether a shake-out is due to manipulation or panicky selling on the part of distressed longs. In either case, the selling that forces the sharp downward acceleration of the price movement is due to supply of poor quality. And the ensuing recovery is caused by the superior quality of the demand which is taking advantage of frightened sellers." — [Course §21M p.1](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Ordinary shake-out and upthrust (§21M p.2):** "An ordinary shake-out has substantially the same characteristics as the terminal shake-out. The principal difference is that the word 'terminal' is used to distinguish a sharp downward thrust which occurs after extensive preparation for a rise and the similar phenomenon which appears at other points in the price movement as, for instance, an exaggerated selling climax". "A thrust movement is the reverse of a shake-out. Thus, a sharp run up out of an area of distribution; or a temporary bulge through the top of a trading range, which fails to hold, is sometimes described as a thrust movement, upthrust or perhaps a terminal mark-up." — [Course §21M p.2](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)
- **Wyckoff's Phase-A sequence wording (§7M):** "should a secondary reaction occur after the technical rally above referred to, and prices hold around or above the climax lows while volume at the same time shrinks appreciably, we have an indication that liquidation was completed and support is again coming into the market. Therefore, the market's behavior on these secondary reactions is usually indicative of the next important move." **Evans' allegories in the most original detail found (all second-hand; no Evans transcript accessed)**
- **The creek / JAC (Boy Scout) story (Pruden & von Lichtenstein 2006):** "One of his more captivating analogies was the 'jump across the creek' (JAC) story he used to explain how a market would break out of a trading range. In the story, the market is symbolised by a Boy Scout, and the trading range by a meandering creek, with its 'upper resistance line' defined by the rally peaks within the range. After probing the edge of the creek and discovering that the flow of supply was starting to dry up, the Boy Scout would retreat in order to get a running start to 'jump across the creek.' The power of the movement by the Boy Scout would be measured by price spread and volume." In their Nokia example the scout says: "Now if I back way up to make a good run for it, I bet I can jump across the creek". — [Pruden & von Lichtenstein 2006](https://www.wyckoffanalytics.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/WyckoffSchematics-VisualTemplatesForMarketTimingDecisions.pdf)
- **Jump vs backup defined (same article):** "A jump is a relatively wider price-spread move made on comparatively higher volume that penetrates outer resistance or support. A backup is a test that immediately follows the jump – a relatively narrow price-spread reaction or rally on comparatively lighter volume that tests and confirms the legitimacy of the preceding jump action. The Wyckoff method instructs you to buy after a backup following an upward jump (a sign of strength) or to sell short after a backup following a downward jump (a sign of weakness)." — [Pruden & von Lichtenstein 2006](https://www.wyckoffanalytics.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/WyckoffSchematics-VisualTemplatesForMarketTimingDecisions.pdf)
- **The creek line (same article / SMI):** "The 'CREEK' is an analogy to a wavy line of resistance drawn loosely across rally peaks within a trading range" (Pruden); SMI glossary: the creek "relates to the flow of supply across the top of the trading range", JAC = "To jump (rally) above the creek (drawn trendline) or flow of supply", and "Mr. Evens finally reached the conclusion that the CREEK is WHERE the BOY JUMPED." — [Pruden & von Lichtenstein 2006](https://www.wyckoffanalytics.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/WyckoffSchematics-VisualTemplatesForMarketTimingDecisions.pdf); [SMI Library](https://wyckoffsmi.com/library/)
- **The Ice Story (same article):** "We imagine the market in the person of a Boy Scout walking over a frozen river in the midst of winter. If support, the ice, is strong the river covered with ice has no difficulty in supporting the weight of the Boy Scout. That support is seen as a wiggly line connecting the lows, the supports, in a trading range. A failure by the Boy Scout to reach the upper resistance level of the Trading Range would be a warning of potential weakness. Weakness of the ice would be signalled by the Boy Scout breaking support or falling through the ice." Also: "Here, the force of 'jumping the creek' (resistance) is replaced by the force of 'falling through the ice' (support)." and "The Boy Scout has two chances to get back above the ice (i.e., creating a bullish 'Spring' situation). ... If on this second attempt, the Boy Scout again fails to penetrate above the ice, he would be most likely to sink downward and drown (i.e., a Bear Market/ Markdown phase would occur)." — [Pruden & von Lichtenstein 2006](https://www.wyckoffanalytics.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/WyckoffSchematics-VisualTemplatesForMarketTimingDecisions.pdf)
- **SMI glossary on ice:** "The ICE is the FORMER SUPPORT AREA at the BOTTOM of the T/R which BECOMES a SUPPLY AREA." — [SMI Library](https://wyckoffsmi.com/library/)

This is good place to initiate long positions or to add to profitable ones." and "the 'pullback to support' after 'jumping resistance' (in Wyckoff terms this is known as 'Backing Up to the Edge of the Creek' after 'Jumping Across the Creek')." — [Pruden & von Lichtenstein 2006](https://www.wyckoffanalytics.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/WyckoffSchematics-VisualTemplatesForMarketTimingDecisions.pdf)
- **StockCharts:** "Backing up to an LPS means a pullback to support that was formerly resistance, on diminished spread and volume." — [StockCharts tutorial](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/market-analysis/wyckoff-analysis-articles/the-wyckoff-method-a-tutorial)
- **SMI glossary, "Backup to the edge of the creek (BEC)" (verbatim fragment):** "is normally a potential (SOS), after jumping the creek the backup to the edge is usually the reaction"; a second fetch paraphrased it as a pullback finding support just above the creek, "often a potential Last Point of Support" (paraphrase, not verbatim). LPS per SMI: "Should have a LACK OF SUPPLY indicated by a RELATIVE NARROWING of the SPREAD and a DECREASE IN THE VOLUME." — [SMI Library](https://wyckoffsmi.com/library/)
- **"Test" in the modern (Wyckoff Analytics) canon:** "Large operators always test the market for supply throughout a TR (e.g., STs and springs) and at key points during a price advance. If considerable supply emerges on a test, the market is often not ready to be marked up." — [StockCharts tutorial](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/market-analysis/wyckoff-analysis-articles/the-wyckoff-method-a-tutorial)
- Source of the modern schematics: Pruden & von Lichtenstein credit "The Anatomy of a Trading Range" by Jim Forte CMT, Market Technicians' Association Journal, Issue 19, 1994. — [Pruden & von Lichtenstein 2006](https://www.wyckoffanalytics.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/WyckoffSchematics-VisualTemplatesForMarketTimingDecisions.pdf)

### Inferences
- Layering of the vocabulary (best-supported reading): **Wyckoff 1910–1932** = test, accumulation/mark-up/distribution, preliminary support, climaxes, technical rally, secondary reaction, shake-out/terminal shake-out, upthrust, springboard/hinge/apex, Composite Man/Operator → **Evans/SMI (c.1950s–1970s)** = spring (from terminal shake-out), JAC/creek, back-up (BUEC), ice/fall through the ice, UTAD, probably AR/ST/SOS/LPS as formal event labels → **Forte (1994) / Pruden (2004–2007) / Wyckoff Analytics** = the lettered schematics, Phases A–E legends, "three laws" packaging, reordered nine tests.
- "Terminal shakeout" is NOT an Evans coinage: it is Wyckoff's own (1932 page); the prior memo should not list it among Evans' terms.
- On BU = LPS: modern teaching (Pruden 2006, StockCharts) clearly presents the back-up as a pullback **to** an LPS (the BU is the move, the LPS its low); Evans' own framing (as relayed by Pruden) emphasizes the back-up as a **test** confirming the jump. Wyckoff's 1931 analogue is the "second springboard position" (shallow, narrowing, low-volume recession after the push through old tops).
- For the screener: the classical lineage supports encoding BU/LPS as "test of the jump" (narrow spread, lighter volume, holding the creek), and the spring as a sub-case of a shake-out test — consistent with SMI's statement.

### Gaps
- No Evans primary material (Evans Echoes audio/transcripts) was accessed; all creek/ice wording is via Pruden (2006) and SMI web pages. The year Evans first used "creek"/"jump" is unknown.
- Exact coiner/date of AR, ST, SOS, LPS as formal labels not established (only "absent from 1937 course; present in SMI glossary").
- SMI glossary entries were partly truncated/paraphrased by the fetch tool; verbatim full entries for BEC, Spring and UTAD are incomplete.

**(f) details — Securities Exchange Act of 1934, Section 9**
- Heading "Manipulation of security prices"; citation "June 6, 1934, ch. 404, title I, § 9, 48 Stat. 889". §9(a)(1): wash sales (no change in beneficial ownership) and matched orders "For the purpose of creating a false or misleading appearance of active trading in any security"; §9(a)(2): "To effect, alone or with 1 or more other persons, a series of transactions in any security" creating actual or apparent active trading or raising/depressing the price "for the purpose of inducing the purchase or sale of such security by others"; §9(a)(3)–(5): dissemination of information that prices will move because of market operations, false/misleading statements, paid touting; §9(a)(6): pegging/fixing/stabilizing contrary to SEC rules. — [Cornell LII, 15 U.S.C. §78i](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/78i)
- The 1937 course footnote itself records the change: "pool operations and old-fashioned manipulation are banned by law". — [Course §9M p.2 fn.](https://archive.org/details/WyckoffMethodOfTapeReading)

### Inferences
- Wording suggestion for (b): "Wyckoff worked in and then ran NYSE brokerage firms whose clients included large operators, knew Keene and Livermore personally, and — according to hostile 1915–1920 press exposés — took part in pool promotions himself (Emerson, Savold); his course presents the operator's techniques as an insider's view ('I am giving you the inside view')."
- Caveat for (f): the statute does not use the word "pool"; it prohibits the techniques (wash/matched trades, transactions for the purpose of inducing others, touting, unauthorized pegging). Wyckoff died (March 7, 1934) three months before enactment (June 6, 1934). A neutral "test" (buy/sell to gauge absorption) is not a named offence; §9(a)(2) turns on purpose — legal interpretation is beyond these notes.
- For (c), the safest memo phrasing: "Robert G. ('Bob') Evans, Wyckoff's student, acquired the Institute in 1951 (SMI), taught through the recorded 'Evans Echoes' lectures, and is credited with the creek/jump/back-up/ice/spring analogies; dates commonly given 1904–1976 (unconfirmed)."
